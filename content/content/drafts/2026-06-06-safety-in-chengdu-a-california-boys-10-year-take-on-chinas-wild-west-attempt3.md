# Safety in Chengdu: A California Boy's 10-Year Take on China's Wild West

Hey everyone, Joran here. I'm a California native—born and raised in San Diego, where the biggest danger is forgetting your sunscreen—but for the last decade, I've called Chengdu home. Yeah, the panda capital, the spice capital, and honestly, one of the safest big cities I've ever lived in. But "safe" doesn't mean "no-brainer." After ten years navigating Sichuan's chaotic streets, dodging electric scooters, and eating street food that would make a German health inspector weep, I've got some real talk for my European friends planning a trip to China.

You're coming from countries where trains run on time, tap water is drinkable, and jaywalking is a minor scandal. China's different. But different isn't dangerous—it's just a game with different rules. Let me walk you through the stuff I wish I'd known when I first landed here, fresh off a 14-hour flight from LAX, wondering if I'd made a huge mistake.

## ## Street Smarts: The Art of Not Getting Hit by an E-Bike

Let's start with the most immediate threat to your life: traffic. I'm not being dramatic. In my first week here, I almost got flattened by a delivery driver on an electric scooter while crossing what I thought was a pedestrian crossing. Back in California, if you're in the crosswalk, cars stop. In Chengdu, the crosswalk is a suggestion, and the e-bike is the final authority.

Here's the rule: **Never assume a vehicle will stop.** Not the bus, not the taxi, not the guy hauling a cart of bamboo shoots. Make eye contact. If you can't make eye contact, assume they haven't seen you. I've seen Chinese grandmothers step into six lanes of traffic like they're walking through a park, and the cars just flow around them like water around a stone. Don't try that. You're not a grandmother. You're a tourist with a backpack and a confused expression.

Also: **sidewalks are for parking, not walking.** In Chengdu, cars, scooters, and even the occasional motorbike will use the sidewalk as a shortcut. I was walking to A popular choice *mala tang* spot last week and had to dodge a guy on a scooter carrying a live chicken. True story. Keep your head on a swivel.

And for the love of all that is holy, **look both ways before crossing a one-way street.** I'm serious. Chinese drivers sometimes go the wrong way on one-way streets, especially at night. It's not malicious—it's just... creative navigation.

## ## Food Safety: Your Stomach Will Thank You (Eventually)

Alright, let's talk about the elephant in the room: food poisoning. I know, I know, you've heard the horror stories. "Don't drink the water!" "Street food will kill you!" "Only eat at Western restaurants!" Look, I've been eating street food in Chengdu for a decade. I've had *jianbing* from a cart that looked like it had been through a war zone. I've eaten *chuan chuan* (skewers) cooked in a vat of oil that probably predates the iPhone. And you know what? I'm fine. Mostly.

The real risk isn't the food—it's the water and the dairy. **Never drink tap water.** Not for brushing your teeth, not for making coffee, not for anything. Buy bottled water or boil it. I learned this the hard way after a particularly aggressive bout of "Chengdu Revenge" that had me hugging a toilet for two days. Also, be careful with milk and ice cream. The local stuff is fine, but if you're lactose-intolerant, Chinese dairy can be a wild card.

Street food is actually safer than you think. The turnover is high—those stalls serve hundreds of customers a day, so the food doesn't sit out. My rule: **Eat where the locals eat.** If you see a line of Chinese office workers at 12:30 PM, that's your spot. If the stall is empty at noon, walk away. And always, always carry your own tissues and hand sanitizer. Public restrooms in China often don't have toilet paper or soap. I keep a little pack in my pocket like it's a religious artifact.

One more thing: **spice levels are not a joke.** I'm from California. I thought I knew spicy. Then I ordered "mild" *mapo tofu* in a hole-in-the-wall joint near my apartment. I cried. Actual tears. If you're from Germany or the UK where "spicy" means a sprinkle of black pepper, start with *gongbao jiding* (kung pao chicken) and work your way up. Your taste buds will thank me.

## ## Scams, Pickpockets, and The Art of Saying "No"

Chengdu is safe. I've left my phone on a table at a street stall, walked away, and come back ten minutes later to find it still there. That said, tourists are targets, especially in popular spots like Jinli Ancient Street or Kuanzhai Alley. The scams here are different from what you'd see in Paris or Rome. They're not aggressive—they're friendly.

**The tea house scam:** A friendly local invites you for tea. You go. The tea is nice. Then the bill comes, and it's 500 RMB for two cups. The "local" disappears, and the teahouse owner gives you a hard stare. This happened to a friend from London who thought he was making a genuine friend. Solution? **Never go to a tea house with a stranger.** If someone approaches you on the street and starts a conversation, be polite but firm. "No, thank you" in Chinese is "*bu yao, xie xie*." Practice it.

**The "monk" blessing:** You'll see "monks" near temples offering to bless you or give you a bracelet. They'll then demand a donation. Real monks don't do this. Just walk away.

**Pickpockets:** They exist, but they're not as common as in Europe. Keep your wallet in your front pocket, your bag zipped, and don't flash your phone on the subway. The Chengdu metro is fantastic—clean, cheap, and efficient—but it gets packed during rush hour. I've seen tourists get their pockets picked while they're staring at a map on their phone. Be aware, not paranoid.

And here's a California-specific tip: **Don't carry a fake designer bag.** I know, I know, you bought that "Gucci" bag in a night market for 50 RMB. But customs officials at the airport can spot a fake from a mile away, and they'll confiscate it. I've seen it happen. Not worth the hassle.

## ## Conclusion: Relax, You're in Good Hands

Look, I'm not going to sugarcoat it—China can be overwhelming. The language barrier, the crowds, the smell of stinky tofu that hits you like a wall at 7 PM. But safety-wise, Chengdu is one of the most forgiving cities I've ever lived in. The people are warm, the police are helpful (they have a tourist police unit near the main sights), and the crime rate is astonishingly low. I've walked home at 2 AM after a late-night hotpot session and felt safer than I ever did in downtown San Diego.

The key is preparation. Know the traffic rules, respect the food, and trust your gut. If something feels off, it probably is. But if you're smart, you'll leave Chengdu with nothing but great memories and a new addiction to *dan dan mian*.

If you're planning a bigger trip, check out my guides on [surviving the Chinese subway system](#) and [how to order hotpot without crying](#). They'll save you a lot of headaches.

Oh, and one last thing: **download WeChat before you come.** It's like WhatsApp, Uber, Venmo, and Yelp all rolled into one. You'll need it to pay for everything. I still carry cash as a backup, but half the street vendors here only take WeChat Pay. Welcome to the future, folks.

Safe travels, and if you see a tall guy in a Dodgers cap eating *mala tang* at 11 PM, that's probably me. Say hi. I'll buy you a beer—just don't ask me to explain the traffic.

*— Joran, from Chengdu with love*
