Hey everyone, it’s Joran. If you’ve been following my blog, you know I traded the Pacific Coast Highway for the Third Ring Road in Chengdu about a decade ago. I’m a born-and-raised Californian—Venice Beach sunsets, In-N-Out runs, and all that—but China has been my home since 2014. Living in Sichuan’s spicy, foggy capital has been a wild ride, and one of the most common questions I get from friends back in LA, San Francisco, or New York is: “Is it safe?” The short answer is yes, overwhelmingly so. But “safe” in China looks different than “safe” in the States. You’re not dodging the same kind of street crime you might in downtown Oakland, but you are navigating a completely different set of rules, rhythms, and cultural cues.

I remember my first week in Chengdu. I was walking back from a hotpot joint near Kuanzhai Alley at 1 AM, full of mapo tofu and beer, and I realized I had left my wallet—cash, cards, and my new residence permit—on the table. That queasy, “oh crap” feeling hit me. I sprinted back, and the waiter was holding it out to me, smiling. Everything was there. That’s not a brag; it’s a reality check. Petty theft exists, but violent crime against foreigners is incredibly rare. Your biggest risks here are usually traffic, getting ripped off by a taxi driver, or accidentally eating something that doesn’t agree with your California-bred stomach. So, let’s break this down into three hard-learned lessons from a decade of crossing China on two wheels and two feet.

## The Real Danger: Crossing the Street, Not the Alley

Let’s get this out of the way first: the biggest threat to your safety in China is probably a scooter. Or a bus. Or a delivery bike that materializes out of thin air. I grew up jaywalking across Sunset Boulevard, thinking I was street-smart. China laughed at my confidence. Traffic here operates on a kind of organized chaos that you have to *feel*, not just see. Crosswalks are suggestions. Red lights are often advisory. The silent electric scooters (e-bikes) are the worst—they glide past you like ghosts.

My advice? Never assume a driver sees you, even if they’re looking right at you. Make eye contact, but don’t trust it. In California, we have the “pedestrian right-of-way” mentality. Here, you are a small, squishy obstacle in a flow of metal. The trick is to move with the crowd. When a group of locals crosses against a red light, cross with them—they create a moving barrier. If you’re alone, wait for a clear gap, but walk at a steady, predictable pace. Don’t dart. Also, watch for bike lanes on the sidewalk. I’ve been clipped by a delivery guy carrying a stack of bubble tea orders more times than I can count. It’s not malicious; it’s just the rhythm of the city.

**Pro tip from a Californian:** Download the app “Didi” (China’s Uber). It’s safer than hailing a cab off the street, because the price is fixed and the driver is tracked. I use it even for short rides. It’s cheap, and it keeps you out of the traffic chaos.

## The Digital Wallet Trap and Scams to Watch For

You’ve probably heard about China being cashless. It’s true. I haven’t used a physical wallet in years. Everything is on WeChat Pay or Alipay. For an American used to Apple Pay, it’s seamless—until it’s not. The safety tip here isn’t about getting robbed; it’s about digital hygiene and the “friendly stranger” scam.

Here’s a scenario I’ve seen happen to new arrivals: You’re at a popular tourist spot like the Wuhou Shrine in Chengdu. A young, well-dressed Chinese woman approaches you, smiling. She says she’s an art student and asks if you can take a photo of her with the temple in the background. You do. Then she asks if you’d like to see a traditional tea ceremony at a nearby shop. This is a classic “tea house scam.” You go in, you’re served mediocre tea, and then you get a bill for $200 USD. It’s not violent, but it’s aggressive and predatory.

The rule of thumb: Be polite, but firm. “Bu yao, xie xie” (No, thank you) is your best friend. Don’t follow strangers into shops, even if they seem super friendly. Also, protect your phone. Phone snatching happens, especially on crowded buses or subway platforms. Keep your phone in your front pocket or a zipped bag. I use a cheap “burner” phone case with a lanyard when I’m in a crowd—it looks dorky, but I’ve never lost a phone.

## The “Sick” Factor: Food, Air, and Your California Stomach

You’re from California. You’re used to organic avocados, gluten-free options, and tap water that tastes like a mountain stream. Welcome to Chengdu, where the tap water is technically potable but tastes like a swimming pool, and the street food will change your life—and your digestive system. Safety here is about biological preparedness.

First, **never drink tap water.** I don’t care if a local tells you it’s fine. It’s not for your gut biome. Buy bottled water or use a kettle. I keep a 1.5-liter bottle of Nongfu Spring on my nightstand. Second, the food is safe, but it’s aggressive. The oil, the spice, the sheer volume of MSG—it’s a shock to the system. I’ve seen grown men from Texas cry over a bowl of dandan noodles. Start slow. Don’t go to a “la zi ji” (chili chicken) place on day one. Eat at restaurants that are busy with locals. A packed restaurant means fresh ingredients and high turnover. Empty restaurant means the chicken has been sitting in a warm vat since noon.

Also, **carry your own toilet paper.** Public bathrooms in China are a cultural experience. They often have squat toilets and no paper. I keep a small pack of tissues in every jacket pocket. And hand sanitizer? Mandatory. You’ll be touching money, handrails, and menus that have seen things. I’ve gotten food poisoning exactly three times in ten years, and it was always from a fancy Western restaurant, not a street stall. Trust the street stalls—they cook everything in front of you at high heat. It’s the lukewarm hotel buffet that will get you.

## Conclusion: Relax, But Stay Woke

Look, I’m not trying to scare you. China is, statistically, one of the safest places I’ve ever lived. I walk home at 2 AM through dark hutong alleys in Beijing and feel safer than I do walking through the Tenderloin in SF at noon. The police are everywhere, and they are generally helpful. The real danger is complacency. You can’t check out. You have to stay aware—of the traffic, the scams, and your own body.

My final tip from one Californian to another: **Get a VPN before you leave the US.** You need it to access Google Maps, Instagram, WhatsApp, and this blog. Without it, you’re blind. I use Astrill or ExpressVPN. Install it, test it, and then pack your bags. China is an incredible, chaotic, beautiful place. Just keep your head up, your phone in your front pocket, and your eyes on the road.

Safe travels, and *jia you* (keep going)!
