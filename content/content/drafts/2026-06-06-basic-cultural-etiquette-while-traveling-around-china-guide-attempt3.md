**Title: How Not to Be That Tourist: A Decade of Chinese Cultural Etiquette (With Movie Quotes)**

Hey mates, Joran here. I’m a California kid who somehow ended up in Chengdu, China, over a decade ago. If you’d told my 25-year-old self, fresh off a surfboard in Santa Monica, that I’d one day be arguing over the price of a panda-shaped keychain in Mandarin while dodging a moped on a sidewalk, I’d have laughed. But here I am. And if you’re an Aussie or Kiwi planning your first trip to the Middle Kingdom, you need a cheat sheet. Not the boring, textbook kind—I mean the real, “I’ve accidentally offended three grandmothers in one morning” kind.

Think of this as your cultural survival guide, narrated by a guy who once tried to haggle for a bowl of noodles and ended up with a free bowl of soup and a new friend. Let’s dive in.

## The Art of the Nod and the Smile (And When to Use Your Inside Voice)

First thing you’ll notice: Chinese people are loud. Not aggressive loud—just *enthusiastic* loud. A conversation in a Beijing hutong sounds like a scene from *The Godfather* where everyone’s trying to out-scream each other, but it’s actually just two friends arguing about where to get a top choice dumplings. Coming from California, where “outdoor voice” is a thing we teach toddlers, this was a shock. I once sat in a Sichuan restaurant and genuinely thought a couple was breaking up. Nope. They were just ordering the spicy chicken.

So, rule one: **Volume is not aggression.** If you’re in a market and someone shouts “*Ni yao shenme?*” (What do you want?), they’re not mad. They’re just excited you exist. Respond with a smile and a nod. That’s the universal “I’m a friendly foreigner, please don’t charge me the tourist tax” signal.

But here’s the flip side: **Don’t use your outside voice on public transport.** Trains and subways are quiet zones. In California, we’re used to strangers chatting on the bus like old friends. In China, a loud phone call on the metro will earn you the kind of dirty looks that could curdle milk. I learned this the hard way when I answered a call from my mom on the Chengdu Metro. The woman next to me gave me a stare that said, “I will end you, and your phone, and your mother.” Now, I whisper like I’m in a library during a bomb threat.

And for the love of all that is holy, **don’t point with your index finger.** Use an open hand. Pointing is seen as aggressive—like you’re accusing someone of stealing your last dumpling. I once pointed at a menu to ask for a recommendation, and the waiter looked at me like I’d just insulted his grandmother. Now I use the “gentle open-palm gesture,” which makes me feel like I’m directing traffic at a meditation retreat.

## Chopsticks, Slurping, and the Great Soup Debate

Let’s talk about the elephant in the room: chopsticks. You’re an Aussie or Kiwi, so you’re probably a pro with a fork. But chopsticks are a different beast. Here’s the golden rule: **Never, ever stick your chopsticks upright in a bowl of rice.** It looks like incense burning at a funeral. I did this once at a hotpot restaurant in Chengdu, and the table went silent. My Chinese friend whispered, “Joran, you just cursed us all.” I thought she was joking. She wasn’t. We had to buy a round of beers to apologize.

Also, **don’t use your chopsticks to spear food.** That’s for forks, which are for barbarians. And don’t pass food from your chopsticks to someone else’s—that’s a funeral ritual too. Just place it on their plate. Simple.

Now, slurping. In the West, slurping is rude. In China, **slurping noodles is a compliment to the chef.** It means you’re enjoying the food so much you can’t wait to cool it down. My first time eating *dan dan mian* in Chengdu, I slurped like a vacuum cleaner. The old man at the next table gave me a thumbs up. I felt like a god. Then Travelers often find to slurp soup. Big mistake. Soup slurping is not a thing. It’s just messy. Learn the difference, or you’ll be that person.

And while we’re on soup: **the bowl goes to your mouth.** In the West, we bring the spoon to our face. In China, you pick up the bowl and drink it like a cup of tea. I know it feels wrong, like you’re about to spill on your shirt. But trust me—it’s the most efficient way to get that last drop of broth. I’ve seen 80-year-old grandmas do it with the grace of a ballet dancer. You can too.

## Bargaining: The Ocean’s Eleven of Shopping

You want a silk scarf? A jade bracelet? A panda hat that makes you look like a giant toddler? You’re going to bargain. And let me tell you, **bargaining in China is not a transaction—it’s a performance.** It’s like a scene from *Ocean’s Eleven*, except instead of George Clooney, it’s a 60-year-old woman in a floral apron who can smell your fear.

Here’s the script: The vendor will quote you a price that’s about 500% more than what it’s worth. You gasp. You say, “*Tai gui le!*” (Too expensive!) in your best dramatic voice. They’ll frown. You’ll pretend to walk away. They’ll grab your arm. You’ll counter at 10% of their offer. They’ll laugh. You’ll laugh. Eventually, you’ll meet somewhere in the middle. It’s a dance, mates.

But here’s the key: **Don’t get angry.** In California, we get passive-aggressive. In China, that doesn’t work. If you get frustrated, the vendor will just move on to the next tourist. Keep it light. Smile. Crack a joke. I once bargained for a *hanfu* (traditional robe) and ended up having a 10-minute conversation about *The Matrix* with the shop owner. She gave me a discount because I quoted Keanu Reeves in Mandarin. True story.

And remember: **If you’re not willing to pay the final price, don’t start the game.** Once you shake hands (or nod), it’s a deal. I once haggled for a painting, agreed on a price, then realized I didn’t have enough cash. The artist chased me down the street. I felt like I was in a chase scene from *The French Connection*. Not fun.

## The Great Toilet Paper Conspiracy

Okay, this is a weird one, but it’s crucial: **Carry your own toilet paper.** In many public restrooms, there’s no paper. There’s just a hose and a bucket. I learned this the hard way in a train station in Xi’an. I was stranded, alone, with nothing but a receipt and a prayer. It was like a scene from *Cast Away*, but with worse plumbing.

Now, I carry a mini pack of tissues everywhere. It’s my survival kit. And if you see a public toilet with a sign saying “*Zhi jin zhi wai*” (Paper in the bin), that means **do not flush the paper.** The pipes are old. Flush it, and you’ll cause a flood. I’ve seen it happen. It’s not pretty.

Also, **squat toilets are not the enemy.** They’re just... a workout. If you’re from Australia or New Zealand, you might be used to them from camping trips. But in China, they’re everywhere. The trick is to keep your balance and don’t look down. Seriously. Just focus on the door. And if you’re really struggling, there’s always the Western-style toilet in a hotel lobby. No shame in that.

## Conclusion: You’ll Be Fine, Just Don’t Be a Jerk

Look, mates, China is not a place you “conquer.” It’s a place you *join*. You’ll mess up. You’ll stick your chopsticks in the rice. You’ll point at someone. You’ll forget to carry tissues. But that’s okay. The locals have seen it all. They’ll laugh, you’ll laugh, and you’ll probably make a friend.

My ten years here have taught me one thing: **Cultural etiquette is not about being excellent. It’s about being present.** Smile, nod, slurp your noodles, and don’t flush the paper. If you do that, you’ll have a better time than I did in my first year—when Travelers often find to order a “beef burger” and got a bowl of tripe soup instead.

And if you see me in Chengdu, say hi. I’ll be the guy with the tissue pack in one hand and a bowl of *dan dan mian* in the other. Just don’t point at me. I’m sensitive.
