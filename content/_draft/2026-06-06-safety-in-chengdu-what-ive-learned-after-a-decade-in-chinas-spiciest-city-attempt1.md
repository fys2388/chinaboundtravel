# Safety in Chengdu: What I've Learned After a Decade in China's Spiciest City

Hey folks, Joran here. I'm sitting in my favorite *chuan chuan* joint in Chengdu's Yulin neighborhood, sweat beading on my forehead from the Sichuan peppercorns, watching the evening rush hour flow past like a river of electric scooters. It's hard to believe I've been here for over ten years now. Back when I first landed, fresh off a flight from San Diego with nothing but a backpack and a vague idea that "China would be an adventure," I had all the usual worries. Would I get pickpocketed? Would I eat something that sent me to the hospital? Would I accidentally offend someone so badly I'd get chased down the street?

Spoiler alert: none of that happened. But I've learned a thing or two about staying safe and sane in this sprawling, chaotic, wonderful city. Since many of you reading this are from Europe—Germany, France, the UK, Italy, Spain—I know you're used to efficient trains, clear street signs, and generally predictable systems. China, and especially Chengdu, is a different beast. Let me share what I've picked up, the hard way and the easy way, so you can enjoy your trip without the stress.

## Navigating the Chaos: Street Smarts for the Uninitiated

Let's start with the obvious: Chengdu is big. Really big. Not "London big" or "Berlin big"—it's more like "Los Angeles big" if LA had 10 million more people and everyone drove a scooter. The first thing that hits you is the traffic. Pedestrians don't have the right of way here. I learned this lesson my second week when I stepped off a curb in Jinli Ancient Street, expecting cars to stop. A taxi driver honked, swerved, and gave me a look that said, *"Are you trying to become a pancake?"* In California, we have crosswalks and signals you can trust. Here, you make eye contact, you walk with confidence, but you also assume every driver is trying to beat the light.

**Pro tip:** When crossing a street, follow the locals. If a group of grandmas with shopping carts starts crossing against a red light, you go with them. They've been doing this since before you were born. Also, never assume a scooter will stop—they're the ninjas of the road. They come from every direction, silently, often carrying a family of four plus a live chicken.

Pickpocketing? It happens, but not as much as you'd think. I've had my phone in my back pocket for years without issue. The real risk is distraction scams—someone "accidentally" spills something on you, and while you're cleaning up, their partner dips into your bag. Keep your valuables in front pockets or a crossbody bag with a zipper. I use a simple canvas one I bought at a local market for 15 yuan (about €2). It's not fancy, but it works.

## Eating Like a Local Without Needing a Hospital Visit

Now, let's talk about food. This is the part that scares most Europeans. "Chinese food safety" is a phrase that makes people nervous, and I get it. But here's the truth: Chengdu's street food scene is not only safe, it's spectacular. In a decade, I've had maybe two minor stomach upsets, and both were from overeating spicy *mapo tofu* at 2 AM after too many beers. That's on me, not the food.

The key is simple: eat where the locals eat. If a stall has a line of office workers at lunch, it's gold. If it's empty at 7 PM on a Saturday, walk past. I remember my first year, I was too shy to try the *jian bing* (savory crepes) from a cart near my apartment. One day, I saw a dozen people queuing, so I joined. The woman making them had hands that moved like a magician—crack an egg, spread the batter, flip it, add sauce, roll it up. Cost me 8 yuan (about €1). I've been going back every week since. Five years later, she remembers my order.

**Food safety rules I live by:**
- **Hot food is safe food.** If it's been sitting out for hours, skip it. But anything cooked fresh in front of you—noodles, skewers, dumplings—is fine.
- **Drink bottled water.** Tap water here is for washing, not drinking. Even locals boil it. Buy a big bottle at a convenience store for 2 yuan. Or do what I do: get a reusable bottle and fill it at your hotel's water dispenser.
- **Spice is not a pathogen.** Sichuan food is famously mouth-numbing, but it's not dangerous. If you're worried, start with *gong bao ji ding* (Kung Pao chicken) or *yu xiang qie zi* (fish-fragrant eggplant). They're mild enough for beginners.

One more thing: if you see a restaurant with a health inspection certificate (usually a green, yellow, or red sticker near the door), aim for green. But honestly, the best meals I've had were from places with no sticker at all—just a grandma cooking in a alley. Trust your nose and your eyes.

## Staying Connected and Avoiding Common Scams

Technology is your best friend and your worst enemy here. China runs on WeChat and Alipay. Cash is becoming rare—even beggars have QR codes. When I first arrived, I had to carry a wallet full of 100-yuan notes. Now, I haven't touched cash in months. But this creates a vulnerability: scammers love targeting tourists who aren't set up with Chinese payment apps.

**The classic scam:** Someone approaches you, looking confused, and asks for directions. They pretend to be a traveler too. Then they claim their wallet was stolen and ask to borrow your phone to call their embassy. While they're "calling," they run off with your phone. I've seen this happen twice. My rule: never hand your phone to a stranger. Offer to make the call yourself, or direct them to a police station. Chengdu has tourist police near popular spots like Kuanzhai Alley.

**Another one:** Taxi drivers who "don't have change" or claim the meter is broken. Use Didi (China's Uber) instead. It's cheap, reliable, and you pay through the app. A ride across town costs maybe 30 yuan (€4). I once took a taxi from the airport to my apartment, and the driver tried to charge me 200 yuan. The Didi price was 60. Learn from my mistake.

**Internet safety:** You can't access Google, Facebook, Instagram, or WhatsApp without a VPN. Install one *before* you leave your home country. I use a paid service (free ones are slow and get blocked). Without it, you'll be stuck using Baidu for maps, which is fine for Chinese, but a nightmare in English. Also, download offline maps of Chengdu on your phone—I use Maps.me. It saved me when my VPN crashed during a trip to Leshan.

**Emergency numbers:** Dial 110 for police, 120 for ambulance. Most operators speak only Mandarin, but if you say "English," they'll transfer you to a translator. Keep your hotel's address written in Chinese characters on a card in your pocket. I've had to use it once, when my friend twisted his ankle hiking at Qingcheng Mountain. The ambulance arrived in 15 minutes.

## Conclusion: Don't Let Fear Steal Your Adventure

Look, I'm not going to pretend China is perfect. There are scams, there's chaos, and sometimes the air is thick enough to chew. But after ten years, I can tell you this: Chengdu is one of the safest big cities I've ever lived in. Violent crime is almost nonexistent. Women walk alone at midnight. Kids play in the streets until dark. The biggest danger you'll face is eating too much *huo guo* (hotpot) and having to loosen your belt.

My advice? Relax. Be street-smart, not paranoid. Learn a few phrases in Mandarin—*xie xie* (thank you), *duo shao qian* (how much), *bu yao la* (not spicy)—and the locals will warm to you instantly. I've been invited into homes, given free dumplings, and helped navigate lost tourists just because I smiled and tried to speak the language.

So pack your bags, download that VPN, and come experience the land of pandas, spicy food, and friendly chaos. And if you see a tall Californian in a Dodgers cap eating *dan dan mian* at a street stall, say hi. I'll probably offer you a seat and a beer.

Safe travels, *pengyoumen* (friends). See you in Chengdu.
