## Tipping in China

### The Basics
In China, tipping is definitely not the norm like it is in the United States. In most restaurants, hotels, and even for services like taxi rides, you don't need to leave an extra tip. It's not that the service is bad-far from it. In fact, Chinese service staff take great pride in their work. But the tipping culture just isn't as ingrained. For example, when I first arrived and went to a local Sichuan restaurant, I tried to leave a tip on the table. The waiter looked at me with a confused expression and actually chased me out of the restaurant to give it back. I was so embarrassed! But this is just one of the many cultural differences I've had to navigate during my time here.

In some high-end international hotels or restaurants that cater mainly to tourists, you might notice a service charge added to your bill. This is usually a fixed percentage, often around 10 - 15%. But even in these cases, it's not really the same as tipping in the US. The service charge is more of a standard fee for the service provided, and the staff don't expect an additional gratuity on top of it.

### Exceptions
However, there are a few exceptions to this no-tipping rule. In some touristy areas, especially those where there are a lot of foreign visitors, you might find that tipping is more common. For instance, if you hire a private tour guide, it's considered polite to give them a small tip at the end of the tour. The amount can vary depending on the length and quality of the tour, but a general guideline could be around 10 - 20% of the total tour cost.

Another exception is in the service industry in some major cities like Shanghai or Beijing. In places like high-end spas or salons, tipping is becoming more prevalent, but still not as expected as in the US. If you receive excellent service, a tip of 10 - 15% is appreciated but not mandatory.

## Photo-taking Etiquette

### Permission
One of the most important aspects of photo-taking in China is asking for permission. This is especially true when taking pictures of people. In the US, we're often more casual about taking photos in public places, but in China, it's considered rude to snap a photo of someone without their consent. I remember once when I was in a small village in the mountains, I saw an elderly woman in traditional clothing sitting outside her house. She looked so picturesque that I couldn't resist taking a photo. Big mistake. She immediately started scolding me in Chinese, and although I didn't understand all the words, I knew I had offended her. So, always ask before you take a photo of someone.

### Public vs. Private
When it comes to taking photos in public places, you generally have more freedom. However, there are still some places where photography is restricted. For example, in some government buildings, military areas, and even certain museums, you might see signs that say "No Photography." It's important to respect these signs. In addition, even in public places like parks or streets, be aware of your surroundings. Don't block the path or disrupt the normal flow of people while taking photos.

On the other hand, when it comes to private property, you should never enter or take photos without permission. This includes private homes, businesses, and even some private gardens. If you're interested in taking photos of a particular place, try to find out if there are any restrictions or if you need to obtain permission in advance.

### Cultural Sensitivity
China has a rich culture and history, and when taking photos, it's important to be culturally sensitive. For example, when visiting temples or other religious sites, make sure you follow the proper etiquette. Dress appropriately (usually no shorts or revealing clothing), and don't take photos of religious ceremonies or objects in a disrespectful way. Also, be careful with your poses. Some gestures that might be innocent in the US could have a different meaning in China.

## Unwritten Rules

### Queueing
Queueing is a big deal in China. People here take their turn very seriously. When waiting for a bus, train, or in line at a store, make sure you stand in an orderly fashion. Cutting in line is a huge no-no and can lead to some very angry looks. I once saw a tourist try to cut in line at a popular tourist attraction, and the locals were not having it. There was a bit of a commotion, and the tourist ended up having to apologize and go to the back of the line. So, be patient and follow the queue.

### Greetings
The way you greet people in China can also be a bit different. In the US, we're often more casual with our greetings, but in China, a simple "Ni hao" (hello) is a good start. When meeting someone for the first time, it's also polite to shake hands. However, make sure your handshake is firm but not too strong. And if you're meeting an older person or someone in a position of authority, it's a good idea to use a more formal greeting like "Ni hao, xiansheng" (hello, sir) or "Ni hao, tai tai" (hello, madam).

### Gift-giving
Gift-giving is an important part of Chinese culture, especially during festivals or special occasions. But there are some rules to follow. Avoid giving gifts that are considered unlucky, such as clocks (which sound like "death" in Chinese) or white flowers (which are associated with funerals). When giving a gift, it's also important to present it with both hands. And if you're invited to someone's home, it's a good idea to bring a small gift like a box of Chinese tea or some local snacks.

### Body Language
Body language can also convey different messages in China. For example, pointing with your index finger is considered rude. Instead, use an open hand to gesture. Also, avoid making direct eye contact for too long, as this can be seen as aggressive. And when sitting, don't cross your legs in a way that shows the sole of your foot, as this is considered disrespectful.
