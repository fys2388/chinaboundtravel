## China Travel Etiquette: Tipping, Photos, and Unwritten Rules

### Introduction
China has changed a lot in recent years, and it's now a top destination for American travelers. But before you jet off to explore the Great Wall, pandas in Chengdu, or the bustling streets of Shanghai, it's crucial to understand some of the unique etiquette norms that govern Chinese society. In this guide, I'll walk you through the dos and don'ts of tipping, taking photos, and other unwritten rules that will help you navigate your China trip like a pro.

### Tipping: To Tip or Not to Tip?
One of the most common questions American travelers have is about tipping in China. In the United States, tipping is almost expected in restaurants, taxis, and many service industries. However, in China, the situation is a bit different.

#### The Basics
In most restaurants in China, tipping is not the norm. The prices on the menu already include any service charges. You won't typically find a line for a tip on your restaurant bill. This can be a pleasant surprise for many travelers who are used to adding an extra 15 - 20% to their bills back home. For example, if you have a delicious meal of Sichuan hotpot in Chengdu that costs 200 yuan (around $30), you can simply pay the 200 yuan and be on your way.

#### Exceptions
However, there are some exceptions. In high-end international hotels or restaurants that cater mainly to tourists, you may see a service charge added to your bill. This service charge is usually around 10 - 15% and is already included in the total amount you pay. So, there's no need to add an extra tip on top of that. Also, in some big cities like Shanghai or Beijing, in very posh restaurants where the service is truly exceptional, some people may choose to leave a small tip, but this is not common.

#### Taxis and Other Services
When it comes to taxis, tipping is not expected either. Just pay the fare shown on the meter. In fact, if you try to give a taxi driver a tip, they may be confused or even refuse it. The same goes for other services like getting your haircut or having your shoes shined. These services are priced fairly, and there's no additional tip required.

YOU SHOULD: Keep in mind that not tipping is the standard practice in most situations in China. Don't feel pressured to tip unless you're in a very high-end establishment where a service charge is already added.

### Photos: Capturing Memories with Respect
China is a country full of amazing sights, from ancient temples to modern skyscrapers. Taking photos is a great way to remember your trip, but there are some rules and customs to follow.

#### Permission
In China, it's generally polite to ask for permission before taking a photo of someone, especially if they are in a private or semi-private setting. For example, if you see an elderly person sitting in a traditional tea house, it's a good idea to ask if you can take their picture. A simple "Ni hao, wo keyi gei ni pai zhang zhao ma?" (Hello, can I take a photo of you?) will usually do the trick.

#### Cultural Sites
When visiting cultural sites like the Forbidden City or the Terracotta Army in Xi'an, there are often specific rules about photography. Some areas may be restricted from photography altogether, usually due to preservation or religious reasons. Look for signs indicating whether photography is allowed or not. If photography is permitted, be respectful of the site and other visitors. Don't use flash photography in areas where it's not allowed, as it can damage delicate artifacts.

#### Public Spaces
In public spaces like parks or streets, you can take photos freely. However, be aware of your surroundings and avoid taking photos in areas where it may be considered inappropriate, such as in front of government buildings or military installations. Also, don't block the path or inconvenience others while taking your photos.

### Unwritten Rules: Navigating Chinese Social Norms
There are also some unwritten rules in China that you should be aware of to avoid any misunderstandings.

#### Queueing
Queueing is taken very seriously in China. When waiting for a bus, subway, or in line at a store, make sure to line up neatly. Cutting in line is considered extremely rude and can lead to angry looks or even confrontations. In some busy subway stations in Shanghai or Beijing, the queues can be long, but everyone waits their turn patiently.

#### Gift-giving
If you're invited to someone's home in China, it's a nice gesture to bring a small gift. However, there are some things to keep in mind. Avoid giving gifts that are too expensive, as it may make the recipient feel uncomfortable. Also, certain gifts have cultural significance. For example, giving a clock is not a good idea because the Chinese word for "clock" sounds similar to the word for "funeral." A box of Chinese tea or some local snacks is a safe and appreciated gift.

#### Body Language
Chinese body language is a bit different from what you may be used to in the United States. For example, direct eye contact is not as emphasized as in Western cultures. Staring at someone for too long can be considered rude. Also, physical contact like hugging or kissing in public is not common, especially between strangers or in formal settings. A simple handshake is usually the appropriate greeting.

#### Language
While many Chinese people in big cities can speak some English, it's still polite to learn a few basic Chinese phrases. Saying "Ni hao" (hello), "Xie xie" (thank you), and "Zai jian" (goodbye) can go a long way in making a good impression. You can also use simple gestures like nodding or smiling to communicate when language is a barrier.

YOU SHOULD: Follow the queueing rules, be careful with gift-giving, be aware of body language differences, and learn a few basic Chinese phrases.
