# Budget Planning for China: A Decade of Lessons from a Californian in Chengdu

Hey there, mates. If you’re reading this from somewhere in Australia or New Zealand, you probably already know that China isn’t the budget travel fantasy it was twenty years ago. But here’s the thing—it’s still incredibly affordable if you know where to look, how to plan, and what to avoid. I’m Joran, a California kid who somehow ended up in Chengdu over a decade ago. Back in 2014, I packed my board shorts and a vague sense of adventure, thinking I’d stay a year. Ten years later, I’m still here, sipping *chuan* tea in People’s Park, watching the same grandmas dance to the same 80s pop. And I’ve learned a thing or two about making a yuan stretch.

Let me walk you through how I budget for travel in China—from the big-ticket items to the hidden costs that’ll sneak up on you if you’re not careful. Because trust me, I’ve made every mistake you can imagine.

## Section 1: The Big Three – Flights, Trains, and Accommodation

When I first landed in Beijing, I thought I was hot stuff. I booked a flight from LAX to PEK for $800 round-trip, which felt like a steal. Then I got to the hotel—a “budget” place near the Drum Tower—and paid 400 RMB a night for a room that smelled like wet dog and regret. I learned fast: the biggest savings come from being flexible and doing your homework.

**Flights:** For you guys coming from Oz or NZ, the game has changed. Direct flights from Sydney or Melbourne to Shanghai or Guangzhou can be surprisingly cheap if you book during shoulder seasons—think March-April or October-November. I’ve seen round-trips for as low as AUD $600 if you’re willing to fly with Chinese carriers like Sichuan Airlines or China Southern. Don’t expect gourmet meals, but you’ll get there in one piece. Pro tip: always check if your ticket includes two checked bags. Chinese airlines are generous with luggage, which is a lifesaver if you’re planning to buy souvenirs.

**Trains:** This is where China shines. High-speed rail is my absolute favorite way to travel. A second-class seat from Chengdu to Xi’an—about 3.5 hours—costs around 260 RMB (roughly AUD $55). Compare that to a domestic flight, which might save you an hour but costs double and involves airport security theater. I’ve taken the overnight sleeper train from Chengdu to Kunming, and it’s like a rolling hostel. You get a bunk, a pillow, and the gentle rocking of the train. Just bring earplugs. The guy in the bunk below me once snored so loud I thought a panda was dying.

**Accommodation:** Here’s my rule: avoid Western chains. You’re not in Melbourne anymore. A clean, local hotel in a tier-2 city like Chengdu will run you 150-250 RMB a night. Use apps like Ctrip (Trip.com) or Fliggy—they’re the Chinese equivalent of Booking.com and often cheaper. For hostels, you can find dorms for 50-80 RMB. I once stayed at a place in Lijiang that had a rooftop garden and cost me 40 RMB a night. The shower was a hose on the wall, but the view of Jade Dragon Snow Mountain made up for it.

## Section 2: Eating Like a Local (Without Breaking the Bank)

I’ll never forget my first week in Chengdu. I was jet-lagged, lost, and starving. I stumbled into a tourist-trap restaurant near Wuhou Temple and paid 80 RMB for a bowl of noodles that tasted like cardboard. I wanted to cry. Then a local friend dragged me to a *mian guan* (noodle shop) down an alley where the sign was just a handwritten piece of cardboard. The noodles cost 8 RMB, and they were the best I’ve ever had—spicy, numbing, with enough Sichuan pepper to make my ears ring.

**Street food is your best friend.** In Chengdu, you can eat like a king for 30-50 RMB a day. Breakfast? A *jianbing* (savory crepe) for 6 RMB. Lunch? A bowl of *dan dan mian* for 12 RMB. Dinner? *Huoguo* (hotpot) with friends, split four ways, for 50 RMB each. And don’t get me started on the fruit stalls. A bag of lychees in season costs 10 RMB. I’ve had entire meals that cost less than a single craft beer back in San Diego.

**But here’s the trap:** Western food. I know, I know—sometimes you just want a burger. But in China, a “Western” burger can cost 80-120 RMB, and it’s rarely as good as what you’d get at home. My advice? Save the Western splurge for once a week. Instead, embrace the local stuff. Learn to order *la* (spicy) or *bu la* (not spicy). Your wallet—and your taste buds—will thank you.

For more on navigating food safely, check out my post on [Eating Street Food Without Getting Sick in China](internal-link). Trust me, it’s a skill you’ll want.

## Section 3: Hidden Costs and Money-Saving Hacks

After ten years, I’ve got a sixth sense for hidden costs. Let me break them down.

**Visa fees:** This is the one that bites. A tourist visa for China costs around AUD $100-150, depending on how many entries you need. Don’t cheap out on the agent—use a reputable one. I once tried to do it myself and ended up with a single-entry visa when I needed double. Cue the frantic trip to the visa office in Hong Kong.

**SIM cards and data:** China blocks Google, WhatsApp, Facebook, and Instagram. You’ll need a VPN. I use ExpressVPN, which costs about AUD $100 a year. For a local SIM, buy one at the airport or a China Unicom shop. 30 GB of data for a month costs around 100 RMB. Don’t use your Australian SIM—roaming fees will destroy your budget.

**Tipping:** Don’t. It’s not a thing here. In fact, some places will chase you down to return the money. I once left a 10 RMB tip at a hotpot place, and the waitress ran after me with the note like I’d dropped it by accident.

**Attractions:** Many temples and parks have entrance fees—usually 20-80 RMB. But here’s a hack: show up early. Some places, like the Wenshu Monastery in Chengdu, offer free entry before 8 AM. Also, get a student ID if you’re still studying. I’m 38, but I’ve used my old university card from San Diego State to get half-price tickets. Don’t judge me—it works.

**WeChat Pay and Alipay:** These are essential. Cash is becoming rare, even in rural areas. Set up your accounts before you arrive. You’ll need a Chinese bank account for full functionality, but you can link a foreign credit card to Alipay now. I remember trying to buy a train ticket with cash in 2016, and the agent looked at me like I was a time traveler.

## Conclusion: The Real Cost of a China Trip

So, what’s the bottom line? For a 14-day trip, I’d budget around AUD $1,500-2,000 per person, excluding flights. That covers decent accommodation, local food, train tickets, and a few splurges like a night at a teahouse or a visit to the Panda Base. If you’re backpacking, you can do it for AUD $1,000. If you want luxury, double it.

But the real cost isn’t just money—it’s time. China is massive. Don’t try to see Beijing, Shanghai, Chengdu, and Guilin in two weeks. You’ll burn out and spend half your budget on transport. Pick two regions and go deep. I’ve spent a decade here, and I still haven’t seen it all.

And here’s the thing I’ve learned as a Californian in Chengdu: budget planning isn’t about being cheap. It’s about freeing yourself up to have experiences that money can’t buy. Like the night I sat on a curb in Kunming, eating grilled eggplant with a stranger who didn’t speak English but insisted on buying me a beer. That moment cost me nothing. And it’s worth more than any five-star hotel.

So pack light, learn to say *xie xie*, and bring an appetite. China’s waiting. And if you end up in Chengdu, hit me up. I’ll show you where to get the best 8 RMB noodles in town. Just don’t tell my mom I’m still eating street food after all these years.
