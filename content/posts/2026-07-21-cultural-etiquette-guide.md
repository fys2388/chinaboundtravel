## Cultural Etiquette in China: A Californian's Guide for American Travelers

Hey there, fellow travelers! If you're planning a trip to China, get ready for an adventure like no other. As an American who has lived in Chengdu for over 5 years, I've had my fair share of cultural experiences, both hilarious and eye-opening. In this blog post, I'll share some of my insights on Chinese cultural etiquette to help you navigate your way through this amazing country with ease and respect.

![Chinese cultural etiquette guide - handshake greeting between travelers and locals](img/china-dest/culture/cultural-etiquette-guide.jpg)

Let me start by saying that China is a vast and diverse country with a rich history and culture that spans thousands of years. From the bustling cities of Beijing and Shanghai to the serene landscapes of Guilin and Zhangjiajie, there's something for everyone in China. However, with its unique customs and traditions, it's important to be aware of the cultural norms and expectations to avoid any misunderstandings or faux pas.

### Respect and Hierarchy
One of the first things you'll notice when you arrive in China is the importance of respect and hierarchy. In Chinese culture, elders and authority figures are highly respected, and it's common to show deference to them through gestures such as bowing or using formal language. When greeting someone, it's also polite to use both hands to shake hands or offer a slight bow.

I remember my first business meeting in China. I was a bit nervous and not fully aware of all the etiquette rules. As the meeting started, I noticed that everyone was waiting for the eldest person in the room to speak first. I quickly realized that this was a sign of respect. From then on, I made sure to always observe who was the most senior in any gathering and follow their lead.

Another time, I was on a bus and an elderly person got on. Without hesitation, several people stood up to offer their seats. It was a small gesture, but it showed how ingrained respect for elders is in Chinese society. As a traveler, it's a good idea to follow suit. If you see an elderly person or someone with a disability standing on a bus or train, offer your seat. It will be greatly appreciated.

### The Concept of "Face"
Another important aspect of Chinese culture is the concept of "face." Face refers to a person's reputation and social standing, and it's considered very important to maintain one's face in public. This means avoiding any behavior that might cause someone to lose face, such as embarrassing them or criticizing them in public. Instead, it's important to be polite, respectful, and diplomatic in all your interactions.

I once made the mistake of correcting a Chinese friend in public. I thought I was just offering helpful advice, but I soon realized that I had made him lose face. He became very quiet and withdrawn for the rest of the day. I felt terrible and quickly apologized. From that experience, I learned that it's much better to offer advice privately and in a gentle way.

In business negotiations, the concept of face is also crucial. If you make a deal that seems too one-sided or if you push too hard, you might make the other party lose face. This could lead to the breakdown of the negotiation. It's important to find a balance and make sure that both parties feel respected and that their face is maintained.

### Dining Etiquette
When it comes to dining in China, there are a few things to keep in mind. First, it's important to wait for the host or eldest person at the table to start eating before you begin. It's also polite to use chopsticks correctly and to avoid making noise or dropping food. When ordering food, it's common to order a variety of dishes to share, and it's considered polite to offer food to others at the table.

I still remember my first attempt at using chopsticks. It was like trying to pick up a grain of rice with two pencils! But with a little practice, I got the hang of it. And let me tell you, it's not just about being able to pick up food. Using chopsticks correctly is also a sign of respect.

When dining with a group, it's a great opportunity to try different dishes. Chinese cuisine is so diverse, and sharing food allows you to experience a wider range of flavors. And don't be shy about offering food to others. It's a friendly gesture that shows you're thinking about them.

### Specific Customs and Traditions
In addition to these general cultural norms, there are also a few specific customs and traditions that you may encounter in China. For example, when giving or receiving a gift, it's important to use both hands. This shows respect for the gift and the person giving or receiving it.

Another custom is the use of red envelopes during special occasions like Chinese New Year or weddings. Red envelopes contain money and are given as a 祝福 (zhùfú - blessing). It's a very nice gesture, but make sure to follow the proper etiquette. For example, don't open the red envelope in front of the person who gave it to you.

If you're invited to someone's home, it's polite to bring a small gift, such as a box of Chinese tea or some fruits. And when you enter the house, take off your shoes. This is a sign of respect for the homeowner.
