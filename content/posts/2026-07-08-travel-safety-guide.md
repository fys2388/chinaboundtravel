## Introduction

Let me give you the short answer first: **China is one of the safest countries I've ever travelled in.** And I've travelled extensively through Europe, Southeast Asia, and North America.

As an American who's been living in Chengdu for over 10 years, I walk home alone at night without a second thought, leave my phone on the table at restaurants, and have never once been pickpocketed. That's not to say you shouldn't use common sense — but the fear that many Western travellers have about China's safety is, in my experience, wildly overblown.

This guide covers what you actually need to know about staying safe in China: real crime data (not rumours), scams that genuinely exist and how to avoid them, transport safety, internet access, health tips, and the essential apps you should set up before landing.

## Quick Answer: Is China Safe for Tourists?

**Yes.** Violent crime against tourists in China is extremely rare. China's overall crime rate is among the lowest in the world for a country of its size. According to the [UN Office on Drugs and Crime](https://dataunodc.un.org/), China's homicide rate is roughly 0.5 per 100,000 people — compared to around 1.0 for the UK, 1.3 for France, and 6.3 for the United States.

What does this mean in practice?

- **Walking at night** in Chinese cities is generally safe, even for solo travellers. Streets are well-lit and busy well into the evening.
- **Pickpocketing** exists in crowded tourist areas (like anywhere) but is far less common than in major European cities.
- **Scams targeting tourists** do exist — the tea house scam is the most well-known — but they're easily avoided with basic awareness.
- **Police presence** is high. You'll see security cameras everywhere and police officers in most public areas.

The genuine safety risks for travellers in China are different from what you might expect: they're more about **digital security** (internet restrictions), **payment logistics** (needing mobile payment apps), and **language barriers** than physical danger.

## Visa and Entry: Quick Overview

**Since July 2024**, China offers **15-day visa-free entry** for citizens of many European countries (including all Schengen Area nations), as well as Australia, New Zealand, and several other nations. You simply arrive with a valid passport and an onward or return ticket — no application needed.

For stays longer than 15 days, European travellers also have access to the **144-hour visa-free transit** if arriving via a third country. For anything beyond that, a standard tourist visa (L visa) is required. For the full breakdown, see our [Ultimate Guide to China Visas for Tourists](https://chinaboundtravel.com/posts/2026-06-02-ultimate-guide-to-china-visa-for-tourists/).

> **Tip:** Your 15-day visa-free clock starts the moment immigration stamps your passport. Have your first night's hotel booking confirmation ready — immigration occasionally asks for proof of accommodation.

## The Scams That Actually Exist (And How to Avoid Them)

Let me save you some anxiety: you're not going to get kidnapped or robbed at gunpoint. The scams in China are **financial**, not violent. Here are the ones to watch for:

### The Tea House Scam

This is the most common tourist scam in China. Here's how it works: a friendly-looking person (often an English-speaking "student" practising their language skills) approaches you and invites you to a tea ceremony at a "traditional tea house." Once inside, you're served tea and then presented with a bill for thousands of yuan. If you refuse to pay, staff may become intimidating.

**How to avoid it:**
- If a stranger invites you anywhere, politely decline and keep walking
- Never follow someone to a business you didn't choose yourself
- If you do want to visit a tea house, research and choose one independently via Trip.com or Google reviews
- This scam is most common in Beijing (near the Forbidden City) and Shanghai (Nanjing Road)

### The "Art Student" Scam

Similar to the tea house scam, someone claiming to be an art student invites you to their exhibition. Once there, you're pressured into buying overpriced paintings.

**How to avoid it:** Same approach — don't follow strangers to unknown locations.

### Unlicensed Taxis at Airports

At some airports, touts may approach you offering taxi rides. These unlicensed drivers sometimes take long routes, charge inflated fares, or demand extra money mid-journey.

**How to avoid it:**
- Use **DiDi** (滴滴) — China's equivalent of Uber. It works with international credit cards through the English interface, and the fare is set in advance. Download it before you land.
- At the airport, follow the signs to the **official taxi queue** (排队打车), not touts in the arrivals hall
- If taking a regular taxi, make sure the meter is running

### Counterfeit Money

If you pay with a large denomination note (especially 100 CNY), a dishonest vendor might switch your real note for a fake one, then claim the money you gave them was counterfeit.

**How to avoid it:**
- Use mobile payment (Alipay or WeChat Pay) wherever possible — [set up Alipay as a foreigner](https://chinaboundtravel.com/posts/how-to-use-alipay-as-a-foreigner-complete-setup-guide-2026-guide/) before your trip
- Carry smaller denominations for street purchases
- This is genuinely rare but worth being aware of

**What you probably WON'T encounter:** ATM skimming is uncommon in China, and violent muggings are extremely rare. The biggest threat to your wallet is usually your own impulse buying at the night markets.

## Transportation Safety

### High-Speed Trains

China's high-speed rail network is the largest in the world and extremely safe. I've taken dozens of high-speed train journeys and never had a single issue. Stations are clean, well-organised, and have security checkpoints at the entrance.

**Practical tips:**
- **Book tickets through Trip.com** or the 12306 app — both accept international cards. For detailed guidance, see our [China High-Speed Train Survival Guide](https://chinaboundtravel.com/posts/china-high-speed-train-survival-guide-booking-classes-and-insider-tips/)
- Arrive at the station **30-45 minutes early** — stations are huge and you need time to clear security and find your platform
- Keep your passport handy — it's checked at security and before boarding
- Your seat and car number are clearly displayed on electronic boards and your ticket

### City Transport: Subway, Bus, and DiDi

**Subway systems** in Beijing, Shanghai, Chengdu, Xi'an, and other major cities are modern, clean, and very safe. Stations have English signage and ticket machines accept cash and mobile payment. Pickpocketing on crowded trains is possible but uncommon.

**Buses** are cheap and extensive but can be confusing for non-Chinese speakers — the stops are announced in Mandarin only. For first-timers, I'd recommend subways and DiDi over buses.

**DiDi** (滴滴) is China's dominant rideshare platform and is generally safe. The app records your route, driver details, and license plate. There's also an in-app emergency button and trip-sharing feature.

## Internet Access and Digital Safety

This is arguably the biggest "safety" issue for Western travellers in China, and it's one that catches people off guard.

### The Great Firewall

Many websites and apps that you rely on at home are **blocked in China**, including:
- Google (Search, Maps, Gmail, Drive)
- WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram, X (Twitter), TikTok
- YouTube, Netflix, Spotify
- Many Western news outlets

**What to do before you land:**
1. **Download a VPN** — install and test it before arriving. Astrill, ExpressVPN, and LetsVPN are popular options in China. For a detailed breakdown, see our [Internet Connection Guide](https://chinaboundtravel.com/posts/internet-connection-china-esim-vpn-guide/)
2. **Download offline maps** — Google Maps won't work, so download maps in Apple Maps or the Maps.me app for offline navigation
3. **Set up Baidu Maps** (百度地图) — it's the standard mapping app in China and works without a VPN

### Payment Apps: Essential, Not Optional

China runs on mobile payment. Cash is accepted but increasingly inconvenient. Before your trip:

1. **Set up Alipay** — you can link your foreign credit card and use it almost everywhere. See our [Alipay setup guide](https://chinaboundtravel.com/posts/how-to-use-alipay-as-a-foreigner-complete-setup-guide-2026-guide/)
2. **Set up WeChat Pay** — for additional coverage, especially at smaller businesses. See our [WeChat Pay guide](https://chinaboundtravel.com/posts/wechat-pay-for-foreigners-step-by-step-setup-and-common-mistakes-to-avoid-guide/)
3. **Carry some cash** (CNY) as backup — withdraw from Bank of China ATMs, which accept most international cards

## Health and Medical Safety

### Food and Water

**Tap water is NOT safe to drink** anywhere in China. Always use bottled water, even for brushing your teeth in smaller cities. Hotels typically provide complimentary bottled water.

Street food in China is generally safe and, in my experience, far less likely to cause issues than street food in many Southeast Asian countries. Vendors cook food fresh at high temperatures, and turnover is usually rapid. That said:

- Look for stalls with high customer turnover (a good sign of freshness)
- Avoid raw or undercooked meat at street stalls
- If you have a sensitive stomach, ease into street food gradually
- Carry **oral rehydration salts** (available at any Chinese pharmacy) just in case

### Medical Care

China's major cities have international-standard hospitals with English-speaking staff. In Beijing and Shanghai, international clinics (like Beijing United Family Hospital or Parkway Health) are well-equipped. In smaller cities, the quality drops but basic care is still readily available.

**Before your trip:**
- Bring any prescription medications in their original packaging
- Pack a basic first-aid kit (painkillers, band-aids, antiseptic)
- Consider travel insurance that covers medical evacuation — our [travel insurance guide](https://chinaboundtravel.com/posts/best-travel-insurance-china/) covers recommended providers

### Emergency Numbers in China

| Emergency | Number |
|---|---|
| Police | **110** |
| Ambulance | **120** |
| Fire | **119** |
| General enquiries | **12345** (citizen hotline in many cities) |

> **Important:** China's emergency services operators speak Mandarin only. If you don't speak Chinese, ask a bystander or your hotel receptionist to call for you. Most hotel staff are experienced with this and will help immediately.

## Safety for Specific Groups

### Solo Female Travellers

China is widely regarded as one of the safest countries for solo female travellers. I've heard this consistently from female friends and readers. Street harassment is uncommon, and violent crime against women is extremely rare.

That said, the usual precautions apply: stick to well-lit areas at night, be cautious with drinks at bars, and trust your instincts. The biggest issue solo female travellers report isn't danger — it's **attention from curious locals**, especially in less-touristed areas where foreigners are rare. This is generally well-meaning but can feel overwhelming.

### LGBTQ+ Travellers

Same-sex relationships are legal in China, and major cities have growing LGBTQ+ communities. Shanghai and Chengdu in particular have active gay scenes. Public displays of affection (regardless of orientation) are uncommon in China and may draw stares — this is cultural, not hostile. For the most part, you'll face no issues as an LGBTQ+ traveller in China's major cities.

### Travellers with Disabilities

Accessibility in China has improved significantly, especially in Beijing, Shanghai, and Chengdu. Major subway stations have elevators, and many tourist attractions provide wheelchair access. However, older areas, rural destinations, and some smaller cities remain challenging. Research specific accessibility before visiting less-developed destinations.

## Cultural Etiquette That Affects Safety

A few cultural norms that are worth knowing — not because breaking them is dangerous, but because they'll make your trip smoother:

- **Don't photograph military installations, police, or government buildings.** This is taken seriously and can result in your camera being confiscated.
- **Don't discuss sensitive political topics** (Tibet, Taiwan, Tiananmen) with strangers or in public. This isn't about "safety" per se — it's about avoiding uncomfortable conversations or, in rare cases, unwanted attention from authorities.
- **Carry your passport** (or a clear photocopy). Police occasionally conduct random ID checks, especially near sensitive areas or during major events. A photocopy is usually sufficient.
- **Don't tip** — it's not expected in China and can sometimes cause confusion.
- **Queue patiently** — Chinese crowds can feel chaotic to Westerners, but there is a system. Follow the locals.

## FAQ

**Is China safe for solo travellers?**

Yes. I've met dozens of solo travellers in China — both men and women — and the universal consensus is that China feels very safe. The combination of high police presence, ubiquitous security cameras, and a low violent crime rate makes it one of the most comfortable solo travel destinations I can think of.

**Do I need a VPN in China?**

Effectively, yes. Without a VPN, you won't be able to access Google, WhatsApp, Instagram, or most Western social media. Install and test a VPN before you arrive — see our [internet guide](https://chinaboundtravel.com/posts/internet-connection-china-esim-vpn-guide/) for recommended options.

**Can I drink the tap water in China?**

No. Always use bottled water, including for brushing your teeth. Hotels provide free bottled water, and it's sold everywhere for 1-2 CNY per bottle.

**What happens if I lose my passport?**

Contact your country's nearest embassy or consulate immediately. For EU citizens, there are embassies in Beijing and consulates in Shanghai, Guangzhou, and Chengdu. Keep a photocopy of your passport and a digital photo on your phone to speed up the replacement process.

**Is street food safe?**

Generally yes. Food is cooked at high temperatures in front of you, and high turnover means ingredients are fresh. The main risk is from undercooked meat or uncooked vegetables — if in doubt, choose items that are thoroughly cooked.

**Do I need to know Chinese to travel safely?**

No, but it helps enormously. Learn a few basics: 你好 (hello), 谢谢 (thank you), 对不起 (sorry/excuse me), 多少钱 (how much?), and 厕所在哪里 (where is the toilet?). Download a translation app like Google Translate (use it offline with downloaded Chinese language pack since the online version requires VPN) or Baidu Translate.

## Conclusion

After more than a decade of living and travelling in China, my honest assessment is that **the average Western tourist is safer in China than in most European or American cities**. The real challenges aren't physical danger — they're logistical: getting online, making payments, navigating without Google Maps, and overcoming the language barrier.

Set up Alipay, download a VPN, install DiDi, and carry your hotel's address in Chinese. Do those four things, and you've handled 90% of the "safety" issues that most travellers actually encounter.

The biggest risk to your China trip isn't crime or scams — it's **over-planning based on fear rather than facts.** China is welcoming, fascinating, and far safer than its reputation suggests. Pack your common sense, not your paranoia.
