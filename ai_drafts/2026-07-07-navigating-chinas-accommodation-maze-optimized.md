## Quick Answer: What You Need to Know Before Booking

**Good news:** Since July 2024, Australian and New Zealand passport holders can enter China **visa-free for up to 15 days** for tourism, business, visiting family, or transit. No application, no fees — just rock up with a valid passport and a return or onward ticket. That makes a short trip easier than ever, but it also means **you need to sort your accommodation fast** since your 15-day clock starts ticking the moment immigration stamps your passport.

Here's a quick snapshot of what you'll pay per night (2026 prices):

| Accommodation Type | Price Range (CNY) | AUD (approx.) | NZD (approx.) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hostel dorm bed | 50 - 120 | $10 - $24 | $11 - $27 |
| Budget hotel (private room) | 150 - 350 | $30 - $70 | $33 - $77 |
| Mid-range hotel | 350 - 700 | $70 - $140 | $77 - $155 |
| Luxury / international chain | 800+ | $160+ | $177+ |

Prices vary by city — Beijing and Shanghai sit at the top end, while Chengdu, Xi'an, and Guilin offer noticeably better value. More on that below.

## Visa Context: 15 Days Changes the Game

Before diving into hotels, let's cover the biggest update that affects your trip planning.

**Since July 2024**, China grants unilateral visa-free entry to Australian and New Zealand citizens for stays of up to 15 days. This applies to tourism, business, visiting relatives, and transit. You don't need to apply in advance — simply present a valid passport (six months' validity minimum) and a confirmed onward or return ticket at the border.

{{< notice "note" >}}
The 15-day visa-free stay **cannot be extended**. If you need more time, you'll need to apply for a proper tourist visa (L visa) before arrival. For the full breakdown of visa options, see our [Ultimate Guide to China Visas for Tourists](https://chinaboundtravel.com/posts/2026-06-02-ultimate-guide-to-china-visa-for-tourists/).
{{< /notice >}}

**For Aussies and Kiwis who want more than 15 days**, you still have the [144-hour visa-free transit](https://chinaboundtravel.com/posts/144-hour-visa-free-transit-guide/) option if you're connecting through China to a third country. But the straightforward tourist visa (L visa) is the way to go for anything beyond a quick hit. The application process takes 4-10 business days and costs around AUD $110 / NZD $120 through the Chinese Visa Application Service Centre in Canberra, Sydney, Melbourne, Auckland, or Wellington.

**Bottom line for accommodation planning:** 15 days visa-free is perfect for a 7-10 day trip hitting two or three cities. Pair it with our [7-day Beijing, Xi'an and Shanghai itinerary](https://chinaboundtravel.com/posts/2026-05-26-7-day-china-itinerary-beijing-xian-shanghai-first-timers/) and you've got the framework of a brilliant trip.

## Which Booking Platforms Actually Work in China?

This is where most foreign travellers get tripped up. The platforms you're used to at home either don't work well in China or charge significantly more than the local alternatives.

### International Platforms (Familiar but Pricier)

| Platform | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|
| **Booking.com** | Familiar interface, free cancellation, English support | Prices are 20-40% higher than Chinese platforms for equivalent hotels |
| **Agoda** | Good coverage in Asia, decent filters | Limited boutique hostel options, fewer local guesthouses |
| **Hotels.com** | Rewards programme, easy comparison | Thin coverage outside major cities |

I still recommend Booking.com for **first-time visitors** who want a stress-free booking experience in English. The premium is worth it for peace of mind. But if you're comfortable with a bit of app-wrangling, the Chinese platforms offer much better value.

### Chinese Platforms (Cheaper, Better Selection)

| Platform | Best For | English Support |
|---|---|---|
| **Trip.com** (international arm of Ctrip) | All-round hotel booking, English interface, price matching | Full English, accepts international cards |
| **携程 (Ctrip)** | Full range of hotels, packages, and flights | Chinese only — use Trip.com instead |
| **美团 (Meituan)** | Budget hotels, guesthouses, and boutique stays | Very limited English |
| **飞猪 (Fliggy)** | Package deals, youth hostels, boutique hotels | Partial English |
| **途家 (Tujia)** | Apartment-style stays, serviced apartments (the Chinese Airbnb equivalent) | Minimal English |

{{< notice "tip" >}}
**My recommendation:** Download **Trip.com** before you land. It's the international version of China's biggest booking platform (Ctrip). It has full English support, accepts foreign credit cards, and prices are comparable to the Chinese platforms — often cheaper than Booking.com for the same hotel.
{{< /notice >}}

### What About Airbnb?

Airbnb **exited the Chinese domestic market in July 2022**. You can still use Airbnb to book properties in China, but the selection is extremely limited compared to pre-2022. For apartment-style accommodation, use **Tujia** (途家) or search on Trip.com's "homestay" category instead.

## The Foreigner Hotel Registration Rule: What You Must Know

Here's something almost no guide mentions: **not every hotel in China is legally permitted to accept foreign guests**.

Hotels need a special licence (涉外接待资质) to register foreign guests with the local police, which is a legal requirement. In practice, this means:

- **International chains** (Marriott, Hilton, Sheraton, etc.) always accept foreigners — no worries there.
- **Mid-range and upscale Chinese hotels** in major cities almost always accept foreigners.
- **Budget hotels and guesthouses in smaller cities** may refuse foreign guests at check-in, even if you booked online.

This has improved significantly — in May 2024, China's National Immigration Administration issued a directive ordering hotels **not to refuse foreign guests** based on registration capacity. Enforcement is still patchy in practice, especially outside tier-one cities.

{{< notice "warning" >}}
**Practical tip:** Always book through a platform that filters for foreigner-friendly properties. On Trip.com, enable the "Accepts Foreign Guests" filter. On Booking.com, this is less of an issue since listed properties are already pre-screened for international travellers.
{{< /notice >}}

After check-in, the hotel must register you with the local police station within 24 hours. Most hotels handle this automatically — just make sure your passport is handy at reception.

## Budget Guide: How Much Should You Actually Spend?

China accommodation is **remarkably affordable** compared to Australia and New Zealand. Here's what your money gets you in 2026:

### Hostels and Guesthouses: $10-24 AUD / $11-27 NZD per Night

China's hostel scene is solid. In major tourist cities, you'll find well-run international hostels (many are HI affiliated) with:

- Dormitory beds starting at 50-80 CNY
- Free Wi-Fi (usually fast)
- Laundry facilities (often self-service)
- Common areas with other travellers
- On-site tour booking and ticket assistance

**Personal pick in Chengdu:** The Mixin Hostel near Wenshu Monastery — a courtyard-style guesthouse in a converted traditional building. Great common area, the owner speaks English, and it's walking distance to some of the city's best street food. I've sent dozens of visiting friends there and never heard a complaint.

Hostels are ideal if you're travelling solo, meeting other travellers, or keeping costs down. But if you're a couple or value privacy, the price jump to a budget hotel is small enough to justify.

### Budget Hotels: $30-70 AUD / $33-77 NZD per Night

This is the sweet spot for most travellers. Budget hotels in China (often branded chains like Jinjiang Inn, Home Inn, or 7 Days Inn) offer:

- Private room with en-suite bathroom
- Air conditioning (essential in summer)
- Daily housekeeping
- 24-hour reception
- Free Wi-Fi

For around 200-300 CNY, you get a clean, comfortable private room that would cost double at home. The major chain hotels are reliable and consistent — think of them as China's equivalent of Ibis or Travelodge.

**Upgrade tip:** For 300-400 CNY, you can often book a "business" or "deluxe" room at a Chinese mid-range chain like **All Seasons** (French Accor group) or **Crystal Orange**. The quality jump is noticeable.

### Mid-Range Hotels: $70-140 AUD / $77-155 NZD per Night

At this price point, you're looking at international-standard comfort. Many properties in this range offer:

- Buffet breakfast (often with Western options)
- Fitness centre
- Concierge services
- Better English-speaking staff
- Prime city-centre locations

This is where international chains like **Novotel, Holiday Inn Express, and Courtyard by Marriott** start to appear. Excellent value for a comfortable, hassle-free stay.

### Luxury Hotels: $160+ AUD / $177+ NZD per Night

China's luxury hotel scene is genuinely world-class and often significantly cheaper than equivalent properties in Sydney, Melbourne, or Auckland. A five-star hotel in Chengdu might cost 600-800 CNY per night — comparable to a basic business hotel in central Sydney.

Notable luxury chains in China include **Aman, Peninsula, Shangri-La, Four Seasons, and Banyan Tree**, plus local luxury brands like **Middleton** and **NARADA**. Many offer exceptional value, especially in second-tier cities.

{{< notice "info" >}}
**Pro tip:** Luxury hotel prices fluctuate wildly based on Chinese holidays. Avoid booking during Golden Week (first week of October), Chinese New Year, and the May Day holiday. Mid-week stays in shoulder season can be 30-50% cheaper than peak weekends.
{{< /notice >}}

## Where to Stay: City-by-City Neighbourhood Guide

### Beijing: Stay Near the Forbidden City or Gulou

- **Best for first-timers:** Dongcheng District (near Wangfujing or the Bell and Drum Towers). Walking distance to the Forbidden City, easy subway access, lively night markets.
- **Budget pick:** Dongcheng hostels — several HI-affiliated options in traditional hutong courtyards.
- **Splurge:** The Peninsula Beijing or Aman Summer Palace (if you want something truly unforgettable).
- **Avoid:** Areas too far from a subway line. Beijing is enormous — don't underestimate commute times.

### Shanghai: The Bund vs French Concession

- **Best for first-timers:** Near People's Square or along the Bund for the iconic skyline views. [Our 2-day Shanghai guide](https://chinaboundtravel.com/posts/2026-05-26-7-day-china-itinerary-beijing-xian-shanghai-first-timers/posts/2026-05-25-shanghai-bund-french-concession-2-day-guide/) covers the best bases.
- **For atmosphere:** The Former French Concession — tree-lined streets, boutique cafes, and independent shops. The most "liveable" neighbourhood in Shanghai.
- **Budget pick:** Hostels near People's Square — excellent value and central.
- **Splurge:** W Hotel on the Bund, Waldorf Astoria, or Capella Shanghai.

### Xi'an: Inside or Near the City Wall

- **Best location:** South Gate area — walking distance to the Muslim Quarter, easy access to the City Wall, and the Bell Tower.
- **Budget pick:** Inside the city wall for authentic neighbourhood feel, or just outside for lower prices.
- **Why Xi'an is great value:** Hotel prices here are noticeably lower than Beijing or Shanghai. A 300 CNY hotel here would cost 600+ in Beijing.

### Chengdu: My Home Turf

Having lived here for over a decade, I'm biased — but Chengdu offers the best accommodation value of any major Chinese city. A comfortable mid-range hotel here costs 30-40% less than the equivalent in Shanghai.

- **Best for atmosphere:** Near Wide and Narrow Alleys (Kuanzhai Xiangzi) or around Chunxi Road for shopping and nightlife.
- **For a quieter stay:** The Jinjiang or Wuhou districts — leafy, relaxed, great local restaurants.
- **Budget gem:** Mixin Hostel near Wenshu Monastery (as mentioned above) or the Lazybones Hostel near Chunxi Road.
- **Splurge:** The Temple House (a Design Hotels property) — one of the most design-forward hotels in China.

### Guilin and Yangshuo: Karst Landscape Base

- **Guilin:** Stay near the Two Rivers and Four Lakes scenic area for evening boat rides.
- **Yangshuo:** West Street (Xi Jie) is touristy but convenient. For something quieter, look along the Yulong River.
- **Unique option:** Cave hotels and cliffside guesthouses in the karst mountains — genuinely one-of-a-kind accommodation you won't find anywhere else. ## Seasonal Considerations for Aussie and Kiwi Travellers

One thing that catches Southern Hemisphere travellers off guard: **China's seasons are the opposite of yours**. This affects both pricing and availability.

| Your Summer (Dec-Feb) | China's Winter |
|---|---|
| Beijing: -10 to 0°C, peak domestic travel for Spring Festival | Hotels expensive and crowded around Chinese New Year (Jan/Feb) |
| Shanghai: 2-8°C, damp cold | Lower tourist demand outside holidays = good deals |
| Chengdu: 3-10°C, grey and misty | Best hotpot weather — and fewer tourists |
| Guilin/Yangshuo: 8-15°C | Pleasant for outdoor activities, thin crowds |
| Hainan/Sanya: 20-26°C | **China's Hawaii** — peak season, prices double |

| Your Winter (Jun-Aug) | China's Summer |
|---|---|
| Beijing: 25-35°C, can be very hot | Shoulder season — fewer domestic tourists than Golden Week |
| Shanghai: 28-35°C, humid | Good value, but pack for serious heat |
| Chengdu: 22-30°C, rainy season | Lush and green, great for panda reserves |
| Guilin/Yangshuo: 25-33°C | Peak season for river activities |

{{< notice "tip" >}}
**Flight routing tip:** Most flights from Australia land in **Shanghai (PVG)** or **Guangzhou (CAN)**, while flights from New Zealand typically route through **Shanghai** or connect via Singapore/Hong Kong. Book your first night's hotel near whichever airport you arrive at — you'll be grateful after a 10-12 hour flight. ## Practical Tips for Booking and Staying

### Payment: You'll Need Alipay or WeChat

Most Chinese hotels — even budget ones — prefer mobile payment over cash or credit cards. International hotels will accept foreign cards, but why pay 3% foreign transaction fees when you can use Alipay?

**Before your trip,** set up [Alipay as a foreigner](https://chinaboundtravel.com/posts/how-to-use-alipay-as-a-foreigner-complete-setup-guide-2026-guide/) — it takes about 15 minutes and you can link your Australian or NZ credit card directly. You can also set up [WeChat Pay](https://chinaboundtravel.com/posts/wechat-pay-for-foreigners-step-by-step-setup-and-common-mistakes-to-avoid-guide/) for additional coverage. Between the two, you'll be able to pay at virtually every hotel, restaurant, and shop in China.

### Check-In Essentials

When checking into any hotel in China, always have these ready:

1. **Your passport** (they'll scan or photograph it)
2. **Your Chinese visa or visa-free entry stamp**
3. **A Chinese phone number** (helpful but not always required — if you've set up Alipay, you already have this sorted)

The hotel handles police registration on your behalf. Keep a photo of your passport on your phone as a backup.

### Review Reading: The Real Signals

Chinese hotel reviews on platforms like Trip.com and Booking.com follow different cultural norms than what you're used to. A few things to watch for:

- **Complaints about "small rooms"** are almost universal in Chinese hotel reviews and usually aren't a real red flag — rooms in China are genuinely compact, especially at this price point.
- **Mentions of cigarette smell** are worth paying attention to. Smoking in rooms was common but is now officially banned in many cities — some older hotels still struggle with enforcement.
- **Staff English ability** varies enormously. If you don't speak Mandarin, specifically look for reviews mentioning English-speaking staff or use Booking.com's English-language filter.

### Booking Timing

- **Peak domestic holidays** to avoid: Chinese New Year (Jan/Feb), Qingming (early April), Labour Day (May 1), Golden Week (Oct 1-7). During these periods, prices spike 2-3x and availability is tight.
- **Book 2-3 months ahead** for travel during any holiday period.
- **Last-minute deals** are common on Chinese platforms — Meituan and Fliggy often have same-day discounts for unfilled rooms, especially mid-week.

### Solo Travellers, Couples, and Families

- **Solo:** Hostels are your best bet for both budget and social reasons. Most major-city hostels offer female-only dorms as well.
- **Couples:** Budget hotels offer excellent value — a private room for 200 CNY is hard to beat. For a special trip, the mid-range sweet spot (400-600 CNY) gets you a genuinely nice room.
- **Families with kids:** Look for "family rooms" (家庭房) on Trip.com — these are larger rooms with a double bed plus one or two singles. International chain hotels are more reliable for cribs and child-friendly amenities. serviced apartments via Tujia are great for longer stays with kids since you get a kitchen.

## FAQ: Common Questions from Aussie and Kiwi Travellers

**Do I need to book accommodation before arriving in China?**

Technically, immigration may ask for proof of accommodation when entering visa-free. Having a booking confirmation (even a cancelable one on Booking.com) smooths the process. For the actual stay, booking 1-2 weeks ahead is usually sufficient outside peak holiday periods.

**Can I use my Australian/NZ credit card at hotels?**

Yes at international chains and most mid-range-plus hotels. Budget hotels and guesthouses may only accept mobile payment (Alipay/WeChat Pay) or cash. Always carry some CNY as backup — you can withdraw from Bank of China ATMs in most cities.

**Is it safe to stay in budget accommodation in China?**

China is generally very safe for travellers. Petty theft is rare, and violent crime against tourists is extremely uncommon. The bigger concern with budget accommodation is cleanliness and comfort rather than safety. Read recent reviews and stick to properties with ratings above 4.0/5.

**What's the deal with "love hotels" and hourly rates?**

Some Chinese booking platforms show "钟点房" (hourly rooms) alongside regular bookings. These are short-stay rooms and not relevant for tourists. Ignore them — standard overnight rates are what you want.

**Do hotels in China provide toiletries and towels?**

Most hotels provide basic toiletries (toothbrush, toothpaste, comb, slippers) and towels, even budget ones. Quality varies. Bring your own if you have strong preferences — and always pack a universal power adapter since China uses Type A/C/I sockets.

**Can I stay in a traditional courtyard house or hutong?**

Yes, and I highly recommend it in Beijing and Chengdu. Several guesthouses operate in restored courtyard properties. Search for " courtyard hotel" or "四合院" (siheyuan) on Trip.com. They're not the cheapest option, but the experience is memorable.
